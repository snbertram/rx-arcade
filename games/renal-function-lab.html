Urine pH & drug ionization

Trap the drug in the urine

Passive reabsorption depends on pKa and urine pH. Only the nonionized form crosses tubular cells back into blood. Ionized drug stays in the urine and is excreted.

Normal range ≈ 4.5–8. Acidify with ammonium chloride; alkalinize with sodium bicarbonate.

Transporter-mediated secretion

Who carries what in the proximal tubule

Tubular secretion is active and saturable, so two drugs sharing a carrier compete. That competition is the basis of several classic interactions. Pick a transporter.

Transporter families

  • Solute carriers (SLC): OATs (organic anions), OCTs (organic cations), OATPs, MATEs (efflux into lumen)
  • ATP-binding cassette (ABC): P-gp / MDR1, MRP1, MRP2
  • Most drug secretion happens in the proximal tubule
  • Protein binding does not stop secretion. Carriers strip drug off albumin, which is how furosemide (91–99% bound) reaches its site

Renal function & drug clearance

Beyond excretion

The kidney metabolizes some drugs

  • Peptidases in the proximal tubule brush border: needed for amino acid reabsorption; degrade peptide hormones and drugs (insulin, enkephalins, imipenem)
  • CYP enzymes (Phase I): limited contribution
  • UGTs (Phase II): glucuronidation makes drugs more hydrophilic for excretion

The kidney clears insulin, so insulin needs often drop as CKD advances.

CKD changes non-renal clearance too

Drugs with <30% renal excretion are still affected. Uremic toxins circulate and:

  • Inhibit enzymes: hepatic CYP and UGT activity drops, half-life lengthens
  • Inhibit transporters: hepatic and intestinal OATPs and P-gp, changing bioavailability and clearance
  • Downregulate CYPs: less gene expression and protein (e.g., CYP2C9)
  • Metabolites accumulate

Furosemide: half-life 0.5–2 h with normal kidneys, ~9 h in end-stage kidney disease, and its non-renal clearance is also prolonged.
